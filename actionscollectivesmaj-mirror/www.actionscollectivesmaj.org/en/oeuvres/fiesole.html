A place for women administrators

A place for women administrators

Jeanne Rhéaume
Fiesole

The inauguration of the Musée d’art de Joliette (MAJ)’s new building in 1976 heralded the arrival of women among the institution’s administration and staff. Carmen Delorme-Toupin, the wife of Jacques Toupin, the Museum’s first secular director, established and led the public programming department. Cécile Martin-Masse joined the board of directors in 1979 along with the artist Madeleine Rocheleau-Boyer.

Beyond her administrative involvement as one of the Museum’s first female board members, Madeleine Rocheleau-Boyer also exhibited her watercolour paintings at the Museum in 1977. Over the years, she became a loyal donor, not only offering her own work to the collection, but also pieces by other women artists, such as Fiesole by Jeanne Rhéaume, in 1979.

Although Jeanne Rhéaume is less well-known than her male counterparts, she occupied a central place in Montréal’s art scene in the 1940s. Trained at the École des beaux-arts de Montréal and at the Art Association of Montreal, she joined the Contemporary Art Society in 1944.1 Together with a group of artists gathered around Alfred Pellan, she signed the Prisme d’yeux manifesto in 1948, published a few months before Refus global.2 Prisme d’yeux, the less radical of the two manifestos, rejected not only overly restrictive academicism, but any narrow definition of modern art, including the Automatistes’ vision.

Still in 1948, a grant from the Québec government allowed Rhéaume to study art in Europe. While in Italy, she was deeply inspired by the Tuscan landscapes. She returned to settle there in 1952 but continued to show her work in Canada, regularly sending her paintings to Montréal’s Dominion Gallery. Through the 1950s, she was a three-time winner of the Jessie Dow Prize, awarded during the Spring Salon at the Montreal Museum of Fine Arts.3

In Fiesole, Rhéaume skillfully and economically captures the soft light and volumes of a small village of the same name, located in the hills near Florence. In some areas, the white of the canvas can be seen through her brush strokes. Rhéaume’s formal concerns, in line with those of the École de Paris, are reflected in the simplified volumes, fragmented surfaces, and accentuated contours. The contrasts of colour, light, and shadow echo the geometry of the architecture and the rolling hills.

  2. Anne-Marie Bouchard, Art moderne du Québec: guide de collection (Québec: Musée national des beaux-arts du Québec, 2018).

  3. Esther Trépanier, ed., Femmes artistes du 20e siècle au Québec: œuvres du Musée national des beaux-arts du Québec (Québec: Musée national des beaux-arts du Québec, 2010), 250.
