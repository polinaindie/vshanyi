  2. This exhibition presents a feminist reading of the Musée d’art de Joliette’s (MAJ) 80-year history through 80 works of art from its permanent collection. It serves as a counterbalance to the Museum’s current holdings, only 12 % of which are works by women. Much like the collection itself, this exhibition features artists who are primarily, although not exclusively, from Québec.

    The narratives that emerge are structured along three timelines. The first explores some of the major changes in women's social and political conditions, as well as the political engagement of artists from Quebec and elsewhere. The second looks at the aesthetic and stylistic choices women artists have made throughout the history of art. The third outlines the history of women’s involvement with the MAJ. 

    The chronology begins in 1942 when Louise Gadbois became the first woman artist to be included in the Museum’s programming, and ends in 2022, the year in which the research for this exhibition began.

    An Anachronistic Chronology Each work in the exhibition is associated with one of three timelines, which guide the viewer's interpretation and provide context. While some works are linked to their year of creation and presented chronologically, others are inserted anachronistically to mark an external event with which they are associated, such as a political demonstration, an important exhibition, or the moment of their acquisition.

    An Evolving Collection Much like the collection they represent, the content of these timelines is not exhaustive; blind spots do occur. Like any museum collection, the MAJ’s is marked by historical constraints and preconceptions. This contextualization allows us to present the collection’s biases, its limitations and omissions, and the ways in which it was developed. Identifying these parameters constitutes a new step toward a collection that is even richer, more diverse, and reflective of women’s artistic production.

    Collective Actions Women have gained ground over the years by fighting for recognition and exposing the injustices they face. Communities have mobilized, and women have come together to gain collective strength in the socio-political sphere as well as in the arts. They have changed perceptions and asserted their rights. This exhibition and the history of the collection it unveils bring to light decades of collective work in celebration of women’s art. This work must continue, because nothing can be taken for granted.
